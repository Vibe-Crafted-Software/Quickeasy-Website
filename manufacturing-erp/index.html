The problem

A good year that somehow did not pay.

Company-level margins can look healthy while a serious share of individual jobs lose money — the profitable ones quietly subsidising the rest. Four reasons why, none of which appear as a line on the income statement.

You quoted it in March and made it in July

The estimate came off a spreadsheet last updated when material prices were different. By the time the job ran, the price you agreed had expired and nothing in the process said so.

The machine was never in the costing

On a factory floor the machine is usually the most expensive resource in the building. Cost labour and material but not machine time and you get a number that looks right and quotes you into a loss.

You bought material you already owned

Stock on the system and stock on the floor are two different stories. Purchasing cannot see what production has committed, so it gets ordered again and the cash sits on a shelf.

Everything gets typed in twice

Quote, job card, purchase order, delivery note, invoice — each retyped from the last. Every hop is a chance to transcribe a quantity wrong, and someone downstream pays for it.

The benefit

Price with confidence. Promise dates you keep. Learn from every job.

Not a longer feature list — three things that change about how the business runs.

Margin known before the job runs

Every quote is built from live material, labour, machine and overhead rates, so you find out a job is thin while you can still reprice it.

Capacity you can promise against

The delivery date comes off a board that knows what is already loaded, instead of off the top of someone’s head in the sales office.

The next quote beats this one

Booked time and issued material post back against the estimate. The costing feedback loop is the part most systems never close.

Quote
